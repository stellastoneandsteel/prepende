# Iran names its price to reopen Hormuz, and the clock in Muscat runs down

*Morning Paiper · August 9, 2026*

Iran this weekend set out the price of reopening the Strait of Hormuz, and it
is a list Washington is unlikely to pay in full: lift the naval blockade,
lift sanctions, withdraw American forces from the region, pay reparations for
the war, and release Iran's frozen assets. Mohammad Bagher Zolghadr,
secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council, said the strait stays
closed until the United States "corrects its behaviour."

The demands landed in the middle of what had looked like progress. Iranian
officials, including Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi, say Tehran is close to
an agreement with Oman on managing the waterway, and Oman's foreign ministry
has described the talks as constructive. But Iran has been explicit that an
Oman arrangement does not open the strait by itself. The 60-day negotiating
window ends in a little over a week. It can be extended. Nobody has said it
will be.

The war has not waited for the diplomats. The United Arab Emirates said this
weekend that one of its ships was targeted by an Iranian missile — a reminder
that the shipping lanes at the center of the talks are still being shot at.
Iran has separately floated banning American and Israeli vessels from the
strait outright and charging other flags a toll to pass. In Washington,
President Trump said the United States holds massive stocks of munitions,
pushing back on reports that five months of strikes have run inventories low.

The timeline behind Monday's talks is short and grim. American and Israeli
operations against Iran began in late February, after Iran moved to assert
control over Hormuz. A ceasefire held from April through a memorandum of
understanding signed in June. In July the fighting resumed, this time with
Iranian attacks on commercial shipping, and the strait — the channel for
roughly a fifth of the world's oil — has been effectively closed to normal
traffic since the war began.

The cost of that closure is not abstract. Brent crude is holding in the upper
seventies and analysts expect it to stay there through September. American
gasoline prices remain elevated, which means the war is being paid for, in
part, at the pump, by people who could not find Hormuz on a map and should
not have to. Every week the strait stays shut, ships reroute, insurance
climbs, and the bill moves further down the supply chain toward ordinary
households — in the United States, in Europe, and hardest of all in the
economies that buy Gulf oil at spot prices.

That is what makes the next few days consequential. Markets reopen Monday.
The Muscat window closes within days. Either the Oman channel produces the
first real de-escalation since June, or the demands published this weekend
become the new baseline and the closure runs deeper into autumn. The people
carrying this story — tanker crews sailing lanes where a UAE ship was just
targeted, negotiators working a deadline in Muscat, families watching fuel
prices eat their paychecks — do not appear on the trend lists. The story
does.
